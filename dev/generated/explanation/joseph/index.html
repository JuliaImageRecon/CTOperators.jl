Joseph's Method

Siddon's Method treats the image as a set of cubes with constant values and computes the exact length of a ray in every cube. Joseph (1982) starts from a different image model: the voxel values are samples of a smooth function at the voxel centers. Within each layer of voxels (see System Matrix), the function is interpolated linearly between the centers (bilinearly in 3D). The line integral of this function along a ray is again a weighted sum of the voxel values,

\[y_i = \sum_j a_{ij} x_j,\]

but the weights $a_{ij}$ are now interpolation weights instead of intersection lengths.

using CTOperators

The idea

Joseph cuts the image into the layers along the principal axis of the ray and takes one sample per layer: where the ray crosses the center plane of the layer, the interpolated image $f$ is evaluated from the nearest voxel centers in that plane (from two in 2D, from four in 3D, bilinearly). Every sample stands for the piece of the ray inside its layer, so it is multiplied by the length per layer,

\[y_i = \sum_{\text{layers } l} \frac{\Delta_k}{|\cos \theta_k|} \, f(\text{sample}_l),\]

where $\Delta_k$ is the voxel size along the principal axis $k$ and $\theta_k$ the angle between the ray and that axis. This is the midpoint rule for the line integral of $f$, with one sample in the middle of each layer. The principal axis guarantees one sample in every layer the ray crosses. The figure on the page System Matrix shows the layers and the length per layer for one ray.

In the figure below a ray crosses a 2D image. The orange dots are its samples on the center lines (gray dots are the pixel centers); the pixels are colored by their weight for this ray. Every sample shares its weight between the two pixels next to it. The slider rotates and shifts the ray: when the ray turns past 45°, the principal axis switches from x to y.

The weights in the figure come from the package: the back projection of the value 1 along a single ray. Rounded to two digits, first index x (rows of the printout), second index y; compare with the same ray on the page of Siddon's Method:

image = ImageGeom(; dims=(8, 8), deltas=(1.0, 1.0))
ray = RayList([-6.0; -2.0; 0.0;;], [6.0; 3.0; 0.0;;])
weights = reshape(adjoint(JosephProjector(image, ray; eltype=Float64)) * [1.0], 8, 8)

Each row of the printout (fixed x) is one layer with one sample. Its two weights, for two neighboring pixels, add up to the length per layer, $\sqrt{1 + (5/12)^2} \approx 1.08$.

Properties

  • Smooth. The projection changes continuously when a ray moves. Siddon's does too, except for rays parallel to the voxel planes, where it jumps as the ray crosses a plane. Rays that lie exactly between two voxels, ambiguous for Siddon, take the mean of both.
  • Exact for linear images. If the voxel values are samples of a linear function of the position, the interpolation reproduces it, and the sum per layer integrates it exactly, for rays that cross the volume from face to face and stay within the outermost voxel centers across the principal axis.
  • Exact adjoint. The back projection uses the same weights, as iterative solvers require.
  • Any geometry. Like Siddon's, the method needs only the start and end point of each ray.
  • Simple to compute. One sample per layer and no decisions which axis is crossed next: the number of steps is known when the ray starts.

The interpolated image is not zero right at the faces of the volume: across the ray it fades to zero up to half a voxel beyond the faces. Segments that begin or end inside the volume (rather than rays from a source outside it) count only the part of the first and last layer they cover, with the value of the sample in the middle of that part: along the principal axis, the image model is constant within a layer.

Coverage of the voxels

Like Siddon's, Joseph's method is ray-driven, and the rays of one view cover the voxels unevenly. The figure shows, for one parallel-beam view with cells of 1 pixel, the coverage (see System Matrix). For rays in the plane at exactly 45° the two methods give the same weights (Joseph already noted this), and the same pattern. At other angles Joseph's interpolation spreads every ray over neighboring pixels: at 17° (0.3 rad) Siddon's coverage varies between 0.89 and 1.05 in bands, Joseph's is 1.00 except for isolated pixels with up to 1.04.

De Man & Basu (2004) report that a distance-driven projector, which integrates over the detector cells, removes the artifacts of ray-driven back projection in their examples.

Implementation

  • The ray is traced in voxel coordinates (see Coordinates and Conventions); the principal axis is the one along which the ray advances most in voxels, so anisotropic voxels need no special case.
  • The ray is clipped to the region where the interpolated image can be nonzero. This gives the first and last layer, and the loop visits every layer from one to the other. Neighbors outside the volume count as zero.
  • The forward projection runs one thread per data element; the back projection adds the weighted values of every sample (four in 3D, two in 2D) with atomic additions.
  • How the rays are distributed over the threads of a GPU is not part of the method; see Workgroups and Ray Order on GPUs.

References
